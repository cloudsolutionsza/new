Beyond the Hustle

Where is the work?

About the book

This preface introduces a book by a development finance practitioner with more than twelve years in the field. The author once firmly believed entrepreneurship was the answer to South Africa's unemployment crisis: talent was waiting to be found, funded and trained, so that township youth could build their way into an economy that had excluded earlier generations. The author no longer holds that view, and the book explains why and proposes an alternative.

The shift was gradual. It came through credit committees, site visits, monitoring reports and conversations with founders whose businesses had failed. Funded enterprises were not failing for the reasons training programmes addressed, and the rare successes were not succeeding for the reasons programmes celebrated. At conferences across Africa, the author heard identical ideas from countries whose economies were equally stuck. Most powerfully, the author met young people who had done everything asked of them (school, courses, business plans, grant applications, start-ups) and remained unemployed.

The central insight is that South Africa has, for a generation, confused the entrepreneur with the firm. Policy aimed to produce entrepreneurs and measured success by counting them. But economies are transformed by the accumulation of productive firms: institutions that raise capital, employ and train workers, adopt technology, build supply chains, create markets and export, together forming industries. Entrepreneurs matter because they found and lead such firms, but the firm builds an industrial economy. Firms need what individual hustle cannot supply: patient capital, reliable infrastructure, large markets, technical skills, and often a state willing to build what private actors will not.

The book's core contribution is Productive-Firm Entrepreneurship Theory, set out in Chapter Eight, its longest chapter, and designed to be both testable and practical. Earlier chapters explain how the idea that entrepreneurship would solve unemployment was manufactured, why it could never work at scale, and what blocks South African founders from building firms. Later chapters examine how other countries built industries and what South Africa must do.

The book speaks to several audiences. Policymakers get a diagnosis of why two decades of enterprise programmes produced little industrial development, plus proposals: a large national industrial bank, a few industrial missions, a cycle of building industries through the state and then selling them, and better impact metrics. Practitioners get a framework for assessing enterprises, an instrument-design checklist and a candid account of the author's mistakes. Economists and students get a theory drawing on Coase, Penrose, Schumpeter, Baumol, Gerschenkron, Amsden, Lall, Rodrik and others, with testable predictions. Young people who want to build something large, perhaps the most important readers, get an honest picture of a slower but more reliable path than the slogans promise.

Written in the first person, the book includes experiences that reflect poorly on the author, who helped design, defend and implement the policies it criticises. People described are composites with changed details, illustrating recurring patterns while protecting privacy.

Statistics date from late 2026, when unemployment stood at 33.6%, with 8.5 million unemployed and nearly half of young workers jobless. The author fears these figures will not improve without a change of course. The preface ends by thanking those who shaped the book, especially the young people whose simple question drives it: where is the work?
